# Mining on Lithos

<span className="badge badge--success">Standard</span>

*For anyone mining on Lithos. Assumes you know what hashrate, shares and difficulty are.*

Lithos is a decentralized mining pool, so it works differently from a pool you connect to over the
internet. The stratum server runs locally from your own client, and your payout is decided by one
number you choose yourself: `diff`.

This page explains how Lithos measures your work and how to choose that number. It stays at the level a
miner needs. For the proof format, the statistics and the contracts behind them, read
[NISPs](./NISPs.mdx).

## How Lithos measures your work

A normal pool counts your shares. Pay-Per-Share and Pay-Per-Last-N-Shares both work out what you are
owed from how many shares you sent.

**Counting shares does not work on a blockchain.** Storing hundreds of thousands of shares on chain
costs far more space than a block reward is worth, and cutting the count by raising the target just
makes payouts wildly uneven.

Lithos measures work a different way. Instead of counting shares, it asks you to prove that your
mining had the shape it would have if you really were hashing at the rate you claim. You do that by
submitting a **Non-Interactive Share Proof (NISP)**: exactly **10 super shares**, which are rare,
high-difficulty shares found while mining normally.

Ten super shares is enough evidence. You do not send the rest, and the rest do not count for anything.

## What `diff` decides

The `diff` in your config becomes your **score** on chain, and score is the only input to your payout:

```text
your payout = the block's reward × your score ÷ the total score of everyone who submitted
```

So a higher `diff` is a larger slice of every block you get paid for. That sounds like it should be
maximized, but in reality this is a trap. This is because of what else `diff` controls.

**Your `diff` also sets how rare a super share is.** Raising it makes each one harder to find. Since
you need ten of them inside a fixed window, and your hardware can only produce so many hashes in that
window, raising `diff` eventually means you do not reach ten.

:::danger Fewer than 10 super shares pays nothing at all
There is no partial credit. A window where you produce nine super shares pays exactly the same as one
where you produced none. Your `diff` is not a target to maximize; it is a bet you have to win.
:::

## The window

You have **60 blocks** to find your ten super shares, counted back from the block you are being paid
for. That is about **45 minutes on testnet** and about **2 hours on mainnet**, because testnet blocks
arrive roughly every 45 seconds against mainnet's 2 minutes.

The block count is the same on both networks, so **testnet gives you less real time** than mainnet
will. A `diff` that works on testnet is conservative for mainnet at the same hardware.

**Sixty blocks is an average length, not a fixed one.** Blocks arrive early or late around Ergo's
target time, so some windows give you far less time than others. The following shows how long windows
lasted from August to October 2026:

| Network | Target length | 9 windows in 10 lasted |
|---|---|---|
| Mainnet | 2 hours | 97 to 154 minutes |
| Testnet | 45 minutes | 31 to 63 minutes |

A short window cuts the super shares of every miner at the same time. The numbers on this page count
both sides of that. You are paid less often in short windows. But when other miners fall short in the
same window, the miners still paid take a larger share of it.

## The trade

Everything about choosing `diff` is one trade: **how much you are paid, against how often you are paid
at all**.

The clearest way to see it is in terms of how many super shares your `diff` makes you average per
window. You need ten to be paid, so the question is how far above ten you want your average to sit.
The following shows the trade on mainnet at a range of averages, so you can find the row that matches
your own appetite for risk:

| Super shares you average | Chance a window pays you | Your score | Expected earnings |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 4.4% | 200% | 12% |
| 7 | 19.9% | 143% | 45% |
| 10 | 55.7% | 100% | 100% |
| 11 | 66.3% | 91% | 112% |
| 12 | 75.2% | 83% | 120% |
| 13 | 82.3% | 77% | 124% |
| 14 | 87.6% | 71% | 125% |
| 15 | 91.5% | 67% | 125% |
| 16 | 94.3% | 63% | 122% |
| 17 | 96.2% | 59% | 119% |
| 18 | 97.5% | 56% | 116% |
| 20 | 99.0% | 50% | 108% |

Score and expected earnings are both relative to averaging 10. Expected earnings is your score
multiplied by the chance of being paid, plus the larger share you take when a short window stops other
miners. It assumes 5 miners of similar size submit to each rollup, as on mainnet in October 2026.

### The ideal `diff`

**Your ideal `diff` is one that averages between 12 and 17 super shares per window.** It is not a single
number a formula hands you. It is the range where miners converge, because it is where consistency and
pay balance out.

The expected earnings column shows why. It peaks at averages of 14 and 15, which differ by less than
1%, and stays within about 7% of that peak from 12 to 17. Across most of the range you give up very
little expected income whichever point you choose. The choice comes down to how steady you want your
payouts:

- **Toward 12**: a higher score, paid about 75% of the time. Larger payouts with more gaps between them,
  at roughly 5% below the peak in expected earnings.
- **Toward 17**: a lower score, paid about 96% of the time. Smaller payouts, rarely a gap.

**Between 14 and 15, choose 15.** The two earn the same, and averaging 15 pays in about 92% of windows
against 88% at 14. The web panel marks 15 as the peak for that reason.

Twelve is the floor of the range, not a target to push past.

### Outside the range

Leaving the range costs you in both directions, and the two directions are not equally forgiving.

**Below 12, your earnings fall quickly.** Averaging 10 pays you in only about 56% of windows. Averaging 7
gives you 43% more score than 10, and you are paid about a fifth of the time, so you earn less than half
as much. At 5 you double your score and earn about an eighth. This is the direction that looks
tempting and is not.

**Above 17, your earnings fade slowly.** Averaging 20 halves your score and pays you almost every
window, and you still earn 8% more than you would at 10. Nothing is lost by staying there briefly, but
it earns less than the range once you know your hashrate.

:::note Steady payouts are the reason to pool at all
You joined a pool so your income would be predictable. Pushing `diff` up until most of your windows
fail reintroduces exactly the variance you were trying to escape, and it lowers what you earn on
average rather than raising it.
:::

## Choosing a starting point

To find the `diff` that averages a given number of super shares:

```text
diff = (hashrate in H/s × window in seconds) ÷ (10,000 × super shares you want to average)
```

The window is 60 blocks: **2,700 seconds on testnet** and **7,200 seconds on mainnet**. The following
gives the floor of the ideal range at averaging 12, the peak at averaging 15, and the recommended start
at averaging 17:

| Hashrate | Testnet, 12 | Testnet, 15 | Testnet, 17 | Mainnet, 12 | Mainnet, 15 | Mainnet, 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 MH/s | 2.25M | 1.8M | 1.59M | 6M | 4.8M | 4.24M |
| 250 MH/s | 5.63M | 4.5M | 3.97M | 15M | 12M | 10.6M |
| 500 MH/s | 11.3M | 9M | 7.94M | 30M | 24M | 21.2M |
| 1 GH/s | 22.5M | 18M | 15.9M | 60M | 48M | 42.4M |
| 4 GH/s | 90M | 72M | 63.5M | 240M | 192M | 169M |

**A lower `diff` averages more super shares**, so the averaging-17 column is always the smaller number.

**Start at the averaging-17 value, then raise `diff` slowly.** Averaging 17 pays in about 96% of
mainnet windows and about 93% of testnet windows, and a hashrate up to 29% lower than you estimated
still averages 12. Follow these steps:

1. Commit the averaging-17 `diff` for your hashrate.
2. Watch how many super shares you produce per window until the commitment may be replaced, 845 blocks
   after the client sent it.
3. If your windows pay reliably, raise `diff` so it averages one super share fewer, for example from
   averaging 17 to averaging 16.
4. Repeat, and stop at averaging 15 or wherever payouts start to skip windows.

Each step is a new difficulty commitment. It binds 125 blocks after the client sends it, and the next
step can follow 845 blocks after that send, about 10 hours 30 minutes on testnet and about 28 hours on
mainnet. The next section explains both rules. The **Difficulty** page of the client's web panel
works out these values from your hashrate.

:::warning Use the hashrate you sustain, not the one your miner reports
Autolykos 2 rebuilds its lookup table every time the block height changes, and your rig stops hashing
while it does. That costs a few percent of a mainnet block and closer to a tenth of a testnet block, so
your effective hashrate is lower than your peak. If your card is short on memory the gap is far worse,
and the [startup guide](../startup-intro.mdx) explains why.
:::

## Changing `diff` later is slow

Your difficulty is a commitment made in advance, on chain. That is what stops anyone waiting to see
which shares they found and then claiming whatever difficulty those shares happen to justify.

It also means you cannot tune it freely. Both rules below count from when the client sends a
commitment:

| Rule | Blocks | On testnet | On mainnet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before the commitment takes effect | 125 | about 1 hour 35 minutes | about 4 hours 10 minutes |
| Before you may replace the commitment | 845 | about 10 hours 30 minutes | about 28 hours |

When you raise `diff`, the client starts mining at the new value 62 blocks before it takes effect. The
first rollups judged against the new value take super shares from the 60 blocks before them, so those
shares are already found at the new value. A super share found at the higher value also counts toward
the old one. When you lower `diff`, the client keeps mining at the old value until the new one takes
effect, because super shares at the old, higher value count toward both.

**Test before you commit.** Nothing is committed until you commit from the **Commitment** tab of the
web panel or turn on `state.autoCommit`, which ships off. Until then, `stratum.forceConfigDiff = true`
lets you measure super share rates at several values without writing any of them on chain. The
[startup guide](../startup-intro.mdx#testing-values-without-committing) covers how.

## What each submission costs

Submitting a NISP posts a refundable **bond** from your node wallet, priced from the difficulty you
claimed:

```text
bond = max(0.002 ERG, your diff ÷ 25)
```

You get it back in full when the rollup pays out. You lose it only if someone proves your submission
fraudulent.

This is the one way a higher `diff` costs you something up front rather than only in risk: at 1.0G you
post 0.04 ERG per submission, and rollups open on every Lithos block, so several can be in flight at
once. Keep enough ERG in the node wallet to cover them.
